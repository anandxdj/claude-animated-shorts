# "The Bridge That Taught a Crowd to March" — script v1 (example made with the cartoon-3d-short skill)
Format: ~71 s vertical short, 207 words (~2.9 words/s including pauses), one steady narrator, our own characters.

## Facts (verify before recording)
- Millennium Bridge, London: steel suspension footbridge from St Paul's (north bank) to Tate Modern (south bank).
- Opened 10 June 2000. About 90,000 people crossed on the first day, up to about 2,000 on the deck at once.
- The deck swayed **sideways**. Walkers widened their stance and swayed in time with it, which pushed it further
  ("synchronous lateral excitation"). Nicknamed "the Wobbly Bridge".
- Closed 12 June 2000, two days after opening. Retrofitted with dampers for about £5 million; reopened 22 February 2002.
- No one was hurt.
- Callback: after the 1831 Broughton collapse the British Army ordered troops to break step on bridges (bridge short).
Source: Wikipedia, "Millennium Bridge, London"; Dallard et al., "The London Millennium Footbridge", The Structural Engineer 79 (2001).

**Everyday analogy:** Everyone on a bouncy castle ends up bouncing together without planning it.

## Script
London built a brand new bridge, and on its very first day, the crowd taught it to dance.
It's June 2000. The first new bridge in central London in over a century, a thin steel blade across the river.
Ninety thousand people turn up to walk across it.
A few minutes in, the deck starts to wiggle. Not up and down. Sideways.
So everybody does what you'd do on a wobbly boat. Feet wider, and sway along with it.
Which pushes the bridge a little further. So they sway a little harder.
Two thousand strangers, who have never met, are now swaying in perfect step.
Luckily, nobody fell in. Unluckily, the whole world watched it on the news.
Every bridge has a rhythm it likes to sway at. Ever been on a bouncy castle when everyone starts jumping? Nobody planned it, but you all end up bouncing together.
The bridge nudged the crowd, and the crowd nudged it right back.
Two days later, they shut it. It took two years and five million pounds of shock absorbers to calm it down.
Back in 1831, the army banned marching in step on bridges. In 2000, a bridge made a crowd of tourists march in step, all by itself.
